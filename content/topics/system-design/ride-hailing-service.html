You open the app, it shows cars crawling around a map, you set a pickup pin and tap. Fifteen seconds later a name, a number plate and a car are coming towards you. You watch it approach, get in, and at the end a receipt appears.

Uber, Lyft and Ola are the well-known versions. The product looks like a map with a button. Underneath it there are two completely different write paths wearing one coat, and most of the design is about keeping them apart.

One-line version: trips are 70 writes a second that must never be lost. Car positions are 125,000 writes a second that are worthless four seconds later. Mixing them is the mistake.

Must do

  1. A driver goes online and streams their position while they work.
  2. A rider asks for a ride from A to B and gets a fare estimate first.
  3. Match that rider with one nearby driver who agrees to come.
  4. Follow the trip through its states, with each side watching the other move.
  5. End the trip, charge the rider, and keep the receipt.

Must be

  • Fast to match: a driver assigned within 15 seconds at the 95th percentile.
  • Exclusive: one driver, one trip. Never two riders sent to one car.
  • Fresh on the map: a rider sees the car within 5 seconds of it moving.
  • Durable where it counts: a started trip survives anything. Positions do not.
  • Uptime: 99.95% for matching. A frozen map is bad; a lost trip is unacceptable.

Not doing

  • Taking the money. We call a payment provider and keep a reference.
  • Routing and ETAs. A map provider gives us road distance and travel time.
  • Surge pricing. That is a pricing model, not a service.
  • Shared rides. Matching two riders to one car on one route is a different problem.

Assumptions, made up: 500,000 drivers online at peak, one position every 4 seconds, 2 million trips a day, peak 3× average, 1 KB per trip row, 50 bytes per position.

That last row is the decision. Keeping every position would cost more storage a day than every trip costs in a year, to hold numbers that are stale before anyone reads them. So positions live in memory, are overwritten in place, and are allowed to vanish. Trips are 70 small writes a second, which one PostgreSQL machine barely notices.

  • The position call returns nothing. No body to parse, no read, no confirmation. A dropped ping is replaced four seconds later.
  • Asking for a ride returns 202, not a driver. Matching takes seconds, so the rider gets a trip id and watches it change state. See WebSockets vs Polling vs SSE.
  • Every state change carries a client event id, so a phone that retries after a tunnel does not start the trip twice.

PostgreSQL holds trips: 70 writes a second, 730 GB a year, money at the end, and a state machine that has to be exactly right. Redis holds live positions, bucketed by map cell. It takes 125,000 writes a second because each one overwrites a single field, and losing the lot on a restart costs four seconds of staleness.

CREATE TABLE trips (
  id           BIGINT GENERATED ALWAYS AS IDENTITY PRIMARY KEY,
  rider_id     BIGINT NOT NULL,
  driver_id    BIGINT,                 -- null until someone accepts
  state        TEXT NOT NULL,          -- matching|accepted|arrived
                                       -- |started|ended|cancelled
  pickup_lat   DOUBLE PRECISION NOT NULL,
  pickup_lng   DOUBLE PRECISION NOT NULL,
  dropoff_lat  DOUBLE PRECISION NOT NULL,
  dropoff_lng  DOUBLE PRECISION NOT NULL,
  requested_at TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL,
  ended_at     TIMESTAMPTZ,
  fare         INTEGER,                -- in paise, never a float
  idem_key     TEXT NOT NULL UNIQUE
);

-- one live trip per driver; the same trick as appointments
CREATE UNIQUE INDEX trips_one_per_driver ON trips (driver_id)
  WHERE state IN ('accepted', 'arrived', 'started');

CREATE TABLE trip_events (
  trip_id   BIGINT NOT NULL REFERENCES trips(id),
  seq       INTEGER NOT NULL,
  state     TEXT NOT NULL,
  at        TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL,
  client_id TEXT,                      -- dedupes a retried phone
  PRIMARY KEY (trip_id, seq)
);

CREATE INDEX trips_rider_idx ON trips (rider_id, requested_at DESC);

trips_one_per_driver is the same partial unique index that stops two patients booking one slot in Doctor Appointment Booking. It is the last guard, not the plan — the next section is about the part it cannot do.

A position ping goes to the location service, which writes it into one Redis cell and answers 204. It never reads, never calls PostgreSQL, and never blocks on anything.

A ride request reaches the trip service, which writes a matching trip and hands it to the matching service. That reads candidate drivers out of the cell index, asks the map provider for road distance, and offers the trip to one driver at a time. On an accept, the trip service moves the state and both phones start watching each other. Ending it is one more state change: the trip service writes ended_at and the fare, calls the payment provider, and the receipt is a plain read of the same row.

How do you index half a million moving cars?

Property Listing Service solved a geo problem with a geo index, and it was the right answer there: ten million pins that almost never move, read constantly. Here the pins move every four seconds and are read a few hundred times a second. An index that sorts positions would spend its whole life being rebuilt.

  • Geo index in the database. Exact, and the write rate destroys it.
  • Cells: chop the city into squares of a few hundred metres and keep one hash per square, driver id to position. A ping is a single overwrite; a search reads the nine squares around the pin.

We take cells. A car crossing a boundary is two writes, one to remove and one to add, which is why the driver id is the field and not the key. Nine cells at city density is a few hundred drivers, small enough to then rank properly by road distance.

How do two riders not get the same driver?

Broadcasting to every nearby driver gets a fast answer and five drivers racing for one rider; four of them lose and learn to ignore offers. So we offer to one driver at a time.

The lock lives in Redis with an 8-second expiry, which matters more than it looks: a driver whose phone has died must not hold a rider hostage, and an expiry is the only kind of lock that cleans up after a process that never comes back. The unique index in PostgreSQL confirms the accept, but it cannot release an offer nobody answered.

What happens in a tunnel?

Both phones lose signal mid-trip, and the trip is the thing we promised never to lose. So the phone records state changes locally with its own event id and sends them when it can, and the server treats a repeat of an event id as the same event rather than a second one. The server’s state machine only moves forward: an arrived arriving after started is recorded in trip_events and ignored for the state.

  • In-memory cells vs a geo index in the database. We take 125,000 writes a second and give up exact geometry and any history of where cars were.
  • One offer at a time vs broadcasting. No driver ever loses a race, and matching takes a few seconds longer per declined offer.
  • Dropping positions vs keeping them. We save 197 TB a year, and cannot answer “where was this car at 4 pm” afterwards. If the business needs that, it is a separate pipeline, not this one.
  • Server as the truth vs the phone. The server decides the state, so a phone cannot lie or skip ahead. The cost is that the phone has to queue and retry everything.
  • Lock with an expiry vs a held lock. An expiry cleans up after a dead phone by itself, and it can release an offer the driver was about to accept.

The order to go in

  1. Scope, 3 min. The five things it does, and that pricing, routing and pooling are out.
  2. Numbers, 5 min. 125,000 pings a second against 70 trip writes. Say the 1,800 to 1 out loud.
  3. Two stores, 4 min. Why positions and trips cannot share one. This is the answer they want early.
  4. Diagram, 4 min. Draw it, then follow a ping and a ride request through it.
  5. Deep dive, 12 min. The cell index, then the offer protocol.
  6. Scaling and failure, 5 min. 10× and 100×, then what the rider sees when Redis dies.

Questions to ask first

  • One city or many? It decides whether anything has to be global.
  • Do we need position history for support and disputes, or only live positions?
  • Who sets the price — us, or a pricing service we call?
  • How precise does matching have to be: nearest car, or best car?

Follow-ups to expect

  • How does the rider see the car move? A push channel over a WebSocket while the app is open, with polling as the fallback.
  • Driver cancels after accepting? Back to matching, offer to the next driver, and count it against the driver.
  • Why not match the closest car? Closest by straight line can be across a river. Rank by road time from the map provider.
  • Airport queues? A cell with an ordered list instead of a hash, so the car that waited longest is offered first.
